A Running Toilet Is a Hidden Water Bill

A quiet leak from a worn flapper wastes water all day with no sign on the floor. The Irvine Ranch Water District's guidance on high bills lists toilets among the first places to look, and says a running toilet can waste up to five gallons per minute.

Because IRWD bills on a water budget with higher-priced tiers above it, wasted water shows up as over-budget use. Fixing the toilet and keeping the invoice is also what a district asks for when you request a leak adjustment.

If the meter moves with everything off and the toilet dye test is clean, the source is elsewhere. In that case, see locating hidden leaks.

What to Do When a Toilet Overflows

An overflow is stressful, and the quick fix is simpler than it looks.

  • 1
    Reach behind the toilet and close the supply valveTurn the small oval handle clockwise. This stops the tank from refilling.
  • 2
    Lift the lid and close the flapperPressing the flapper down in the tank stops more water from entering the bowl.
  • 3
    Do not flush againAnother flush adds more water to a bowl that cannot drain.
  • 4
    Contain the waterUse towels to block it from spreading, and keep children and pets away from the water.
  • 5
    Call if the drain is blockedA repeating overflow usually means a clog below the toilet. See drain cleaning and unclogging.

Replacing a Toilet in a Lake Forest Home

California limits new toilets to 1.28 gallons per flush, so a replacement saves water compared with older models. That matters in the 1970s and 1980s homes of the El Toro core, where original toilets may use far more per flush.

Before buying a toilet, measure the rough-in, which is the distance from the wall to the center of the drain. Twelve inches is the common size, but ten and fourteen inches exist. The closet flange on the floor should be sound and sit at the right height, and a new wax ring or a wax-free seal goes on it.

In condos and townhomes, check with your association about work on shared water lines and any rules for shutting off water. A leak from an upstairs toilet can reach the unit below, which is covered under water extraction and drying if damage has spread.

How a Toilet Replacement Goes

  1. Shut off and drain

    The plumber closes the supply and empties the tank and bowl.

  2. Remove the old toilet

    The plumber loosens the bolts, lifts the toilet, and clears the old wax ring.

  3. Check the flange

    The plumber inspects the flange and the floor around it for damage or rot.

  4. Set and test

    The plumber sets a new seal and the toilet, reconnects the supply, and tests the unit for leaks.
