Pinhole Leaks, Rusty Water, and Low Pressure Everywhere: Is It Time to Repipe?

Repiping is a big decision, and most homeowners start with one symptom. When several of these show up together, the pipes usually show wear throughout, not at one spot.

Repeated pinhole leaks

A second or third leak in a year means the pipe wall is thinning along its length.

Rust-colored or metallic water

Brown water that clears after running points to corroding steel or old pipe interiors.

Low pressure at several fixtures

Mineral buildup or corrosion inside pipes narrows them over time.

Green stains on fixtures or at joints

Corroding copper leaves a blue-green residue.

Water stains on ceilings or walls

Several unexplained stains mean leaks in more than one line.

A home with a known problem pipe

Some pipe materials have a history of failing, as covered below.

Repiping Versus Water Line Replacement

People often confuse the two jobs. Repiping replaces the water supply lines inside the house, from the main shutoff to every fixture. Water service line replacement covers the buried pipe from the district's meter to the house.

A failing line outside the house shows up as a soggy yard or a meter that spins with the house off. That is covered under water line repair and replacement. Pipes inside walls and ceilings are the subject of this page.

Some homes need both, and the order matters. Replacing the inside pipes without testing the service line can leave a weak link outside the house.

Pipe Materials and What They Mean for a Home

The age of the house is a clue to the pipe material. A plumber confirms by looking, not by assuming.

MaterialCommon inWhat to know
Galvanized steelHomes from before the 1960sCorrodes from inside, narrowing the pipe and causing low pressure
CopperHomes from the 1960s through todayLasts decades, but pinhole leaks can develop where water chemistry is aggressive
Polybutylene (gray plastic)Some homes built from the late 1970s through the mid 1990sKnown for failing, and many homeowners replace it
PEXNewer homes and repipesFlexible, resists corrosion, and installs with fewer joints

If your home was built in the polybutylene window, ask whether it was already repiped. Many were.

What the Housing Eras Mean for Lake Forest Pipes

Builders built the older El Toro and Lake Forest Drive neighborhoods mostly in the 1970s and 1980s, so the original supply pipes are now forty to fifty years old. Foothill Ranch and Portola Hills date to the 1990s, and Baker Ranch to the 2010s, so their pipes are younger and less likely to need replacement.

Water chemistry also matters. Published hardness figures for the area run from moderately hard to hard, and the districts treat the water with disinfectants. A plumber can look at the inside of a cut pipe and tell you whether scale or corrosion is the issue.

Before repiping, ask whether a pressure regulator is working. Pressure above 80 psi stresses even new pipes, and the California Plumbing Code, built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), limits it for that reason.

How a Repipe Is Done

  1. Inspect

    Pipe material, pressure, and access are checked, and the scope is measured.

  2. Quote and plan

    You see the price, the material, the permit plan, and how long the water will be off.

  3. Install

    New lines are run through the attic, walls, or crawlspace, with small access holes cut where needed.

  4. Test and inspect

    The system is pressure tested and inspected, and drywall patches are left ready.
