Should You Get a Sewer Camera Inspection Before Buying a Home in Orange County?

By the Lake Forest Pro Plumbing team. Published October 3, 2026. Updated October 3, 2026.

A sewer camera inspection sends a small camera through the lateral from the house to the street and records cracks, roots, offsets, and sags. It is worth ordering for homes built before the 1990s, because the lateral is generally the owner's responsibility and repair or replacement can be costly. Ask for the video and a written report before your inspection period ends.

What a Sewer Scope Shows

A standard home inspection looks at what can be seen, and a lateral is buried. A camera on a flexible cable travels from a cleanout toward the street and shows the inside of the pipe on video. A good operator notes the distance to each defect, the pipe material and size, and whether water flows or pools.

That tells you whether the pipe is sound, whether roots are present, and whether a section has cracked, offset, or sagged. It is the only way to see those things before they become a backup in your first year of ownership.

What Findings Mean for the Deal

Clean, round pipeNo visible defectsNo action needed

What to Ask the Inspector For

Not every scope report is equally useful. These items make it usable.

  • 1
    The full videoNot just a summary. A second opinion needs the recording.
  • 2
    Distances from the cleanoutEach defect should be located in feet.
  • 3
    Pipe material and diameterMaterial decides what repair options exist.
  • 4
    A statement of what could not be seenA blocked line or a missing cleanout limits the scope.
  • 5
    A recommendationThe report should say what, if anything, needs doing.

Which Homes Most Need One

Age is the main factor. Lake Forest homes from the 1960s through the 1980s have laterals that are four to six decades old, and mature trees add risk. Homes built after 2010, such as those in Baker Ranch, have much younger pipes and a lower chance of trouble.

Shared laterals add a layer. In townhome clusters and in a community such as Laguna Woods, the lateral may belong to an association or a mutual, so ask who owns and maintains it before you rely on a scope of your own unit.

The findings feed directly into the repair choices described under sewer line repair and sewer line replacement.

Using the Report in Negotiation

If the scope finds a problem, you have options: ask the seller to repair it, ask for a credit, or renegotiate the price. A written quote from a plumber for the repair makes the conversation concrete. Your agent can advise on how and when to raise it within your inspection contingency.

If the scope is clean, you have a baseline. Keep the video. If you later have a backup, you can compare it with what the pipe looked like when you bought the house.

Where It Fits in the Timeline

  1. Order early

    Schedule the scope soon after your offer is accepted, since inspection periods are short.

  2. Get the report

    Ask for the video and the written summary as soon as it is ready.

  3. Price any repair

    Get a quote from a plumber for anything the scope found.

  4. Decide with your agent

    Choose whether to ask for a repair, a credit, or to proceed.

What It Costs and How Long It Takes

A sewer scope is a modest cost compared with a home purchase, and far less than a replacement. Prices vary by company and by how accessible the cleanout is, so ask for a written price before you book. A straightforward scope takes a short visit, and the report usually follows soon after.

Be wary of an inspection that is so cheap it leaves out the report, or one that offers to fix whatever it finds. An inspection and a repair sale are different jobs, and a buyer is better served by an independent report.

What a Scope Cannot Tell You

A camera sees the inside of the pipe it can reach. It cannot see through a blockage, and it cannot always pass a sharp bend or a collapsed section. It does not measure how much flow the pipe can handle, and it does not examine the main in the street, which belongs to the district.

A clean scope is a good sign and not a guarantee. Roots can grow into an opening after the scope, and pipe can shift. The video is a snapshot of one day, which is why keeping it matters.

Questions to Ask the Seller

Sellers know things an inspector cannot see. A few questions are worth asking.

  • 1
    Has the sewer ever backed up?Ask when, how often, and what was done.
  • 2
    Have roots ever been cleared?A history of root clearing points to an opening.
  • 3
    Where is the cleanout?Knowing the location makes the scope easier.
  • 4
    Has the lateral been repaired or lined?Ask for the invoices and any warranty.
  • 5
    Who owns the lateral?In a shared or managed community, the answer may not be the unit owner.

Newer Homes Still Deserve a Look

A home that is ten or fifteen years old has a young lateral, but young pipe can still have a defect from installation, such as an offset joint or a low spot. A scope on a newer home is quick and usually clean, and it is a cheap way to confirm that.

If the home is still within a builder's warranty period, a defect found early may be the builder's responsibility. Photograph the finding and ask your agent or an attorney how to raise it before you pay for a repair yourself.

Reading the Video Like a Buyer

You do not need to be an expert to watch the footage. A healthy pipe looks like a continuous round tunnel with faint lines at the joints. Water that pools in a low spot and does not drain means a sag. A pale or brown hairy mass is usually roots, and a rough, flaking surface in a cast iron pipe is scale.

Watch the distance counter, which shows how far the camera has traveled. Ask the operator to pause and label each defect with its distance and type, so the report matches the video. A crack, an offset joint where one pipe end does not meet the next, and a collapse look different and cost differently, which is why the labels matter.
