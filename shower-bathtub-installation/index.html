What Goes Behind the Wall

The valve is the part that matters most for safety. California plumbing code requires shower valves that protect against scalding, either pressure-balanced or thermostatic, so a toilet flush elsewhere in the house does not send the water to scalding temperature. The California Plumbing Code is built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC).

Waterproofing is the part that fails most often. A shower pan or membrane that leaks sends water into the framing and floor, where it may go unseen for months. An inspector will commonly require a flood test of the shower pan before anyone closes the walls.

A leak behind a finished shower can be found without opening the wall, and leak detection explains how. If water has already damaged the structure, see water mitigation.

Flow, Heat, and Hot Water: Fit the Fixture to the House

California limits new showerheads to 1.8 gallons per minute under its green building code. A new shower will not use more water than that, whatever the showerhead's marketing says. Multiple body sprays can add up, so check the combined flow if you plan a multi-head system.

A deep soaking tub can hold fifty gallons or more, and a standard 40 or 50 gallon water heater may not keep up. If the tank runs out before the tub fills, the plan may need a larger heater, covered under water heater installation and replacement.

Water pressure affects the experience. High pressure above 80 psi stresses valves, so a regulator belongs in the plan. Low pressure may mean the supply lines are worn, which is a different project.

How a Shower or Tub Installation Goes

  1. Plan and quote

    The plumber settles and prices the fixtures, valve, drain position, and permit needs.

  2. Remove and rough in

    The old fixture comes out and the new valve, supply lines, and drain are set.

  3. Test and inspect

    Supply lines are pressure tested, the shower pan is flood tested, and the inspector checks the rough-in.

  4. Finish and test

    The fixture is set, the trim is installed, and everything is tested for leaks and temperature.

In the 1970s and 1980s homes of the El Toro core, original tubs and valves are now four to five decades old. Older valves may use separate hot and cold handles with no scald protection, and replacing them to current code is a common reason for the project.
